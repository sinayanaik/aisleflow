Aisleflow — lifelong priority inheritance with backtracking, on a warehouse grid. Four score terms, thirty-seven parameters, and a movement layer that never removes a legal move.

Set the frame before any detail: this is a review of a research implementation, and the honest headline has two halves. On the map where corridors are the constraint it beats every published planner we could run. On open floors, plain lifelong PIBT with a turning cost beats it — and the deck shows that measurement rather than burying it.

The numbers on this slide are generated from the code and the dataset at build time, like every other number in the deck. Nothing here is typed by hand.

What this deck covers

Three questions

  1. Where did MAPF come from?

    From decoupled prioritised planning in 1987, through optimal solvers, to the greedy planners that made thousands of robots tractable.

  2. How does this planner actually decide?

    Three computations, one per slide block, each worked through on a real floor with the real numbers: which robot takes which task, which cell a robot moves into, and who gets to move first.

  3. What survived being measured?

    Nine score terms became four; sixty parameters became thirty-seven; a whole aisle-direction layer was deleted. Every deletion has a measurement behind it, and they are all in here.

The design principle that runs through all three: congestion, lane preference and turning cost decide which legal move PIBT tries first — they never replace its collision checks, and they never remove a candidate.

Flag the third bullet now rather than at the end. An audience told at the start that most of the project was deleted listens to the evidence; an audience told at slide 30 listens for what else was hidden.

The problem

Multi-agent path finding

Given a graph and k agents, each with a start and a goal, find k paths such that every agent arrives and no two agents ever collide.

Time is discrete. At each timestep an agent moves along one edge or waits. Everything else in MAPF — makespan, sum-of-costs, or the throughput objective this project uses — is an optimisation layered on top of that one rule.

Two kinds of collision define the problem. They are the next slide, and they are the only two things this planner checks before it lets a robot move.

Keep this slide short on purpose. The definition is not the interesting part; the two collision types on the next slide are, because the entire architectural argument later ("scoring never decides safety") is an argument about who is allowed to enforce exactly those two rules.

The problem

Two collisions, and nothing else

Vertex conflict

Two robots in the same cell at the same timestep. Checked in O(1) against the set of cells already claimed this step.

Swap conflict

Two robots exchange cells across one edge in one timestep. The one people forget, because each move is individually legal.

Following is not a conflict

A robot may take the cell another is leaving. Forbidding this — some planners do — turns every queue into a stall.

These two checks are the only hard rules in the movement layer. Every preference in the rest of this deck is a score, and a score can never reject a move.

The swap conflict is the one people forget. Each robot's move is individually legal — each is moving into a cell the other is vacating — and only the pair is illegal. It is also the one that makes a naive "check the destination is empty" implementation wrong.

The third panel is worth a beat because it is the difference between a corridor that drains and one that gridlocks. Token Passing completes zero tasks on one of our maps, and the queue it cannot resolve is exactly this picture.

Why it resisted forty years of attack · 1 of 3

The joint action space is exponential in the agents

Each agent may move in four directions or wait. For k agents planned together, the joint branching factor is 5k.

Coupled search is therefore exponential in the number of agents, not in the size of the map. Doubling the floor space is cheap; adding ten robots is not.

≈5k
joint branching factor

Concretely: ten agents is about ten million joint actions per timestep. This is the number that pushed the field towards decoupling, and it is the number PIBT sidesteps by never planning more than one timestep ahead.

Why it resisted forty years of attack · 2 of 3

Optimal MAPF is NP-hard

NP-hard for both makespan and sum-of-costs (Yu & LaValle, 2013), anticipated by Ratner & Warmuth on the generalised 15-puzzle in 1986.

So optimality is not a matter of finding a cleverer algorithm. Any planner that scales is giving something up, and the interesting question is which thing.

NP-hard
to solve optimally

Do not linger. The point is only to establish that the tradeoff table two slides on is forced, not chosen.

Why it resisted forty years of attack · 3 of 3

In a warehouse, the horizon never ends

A delivered task is immediately replaced by a new one. There is no final goal configuration to plan to.

The classical formulation does not get harder here. It stops applying. This is the turn that made lifelong MAPD a separate field, and it is the setting this project lives in.

Endless
no terminal state
So the field split. One branch chased optimality and paid in scale. One gave up optimality for completeness. A third gave up both for speed — and that turned out to be the branch warehouses needed.

This slide is the hinge of Act 1. Everything before it is classical MAPF; everything after it is about what you do when there is no plan to finish.

History · era 1 of 5

Decoupled: plan one agent at a time

Erdmann & Lozano-Pérez, 1987

Plan each agent in turn, treating the already-planned ones as moving obstacles.

Silver's Cooperative A* / WHCA*, 2005

Adds a shared reservation table over space-time, so later agents can see where earlier ones will be.

Fast, incomplete, and sensitive to the planning order — the ordering problem that priority inheritance later attacks directly.

The through-line to flag: this era's weakness is that the priority order is fixed in advance. PIBT's contribution, twenty years later, is letting the order change during the timestep.

History · era 2 of 5

Optimal: search the conflicts, not the joint space

CBS — Sharon et al.

Find a conflict, split on it, and re-plan the two single agents under the resulting constraints. Optimal and genuinely elegant.

ICTS, M*, branch-and-cut-and-price

Different decompositions of the same idea, all following within a few years.

And it falls over past tens of agents in dense maps: the conflict tree grows with the conflicts, and a dense warehouse is nothing but conflicts.

If asked why we did not benchmark against CBS: at the densities in this project it does not finish. The three baselines we do run — Token Passing, TPTS and RHCR — are the lifelong ones, which is the honest comparison class.

History · era 3 of 5

Rule-based: complete by construction

Push and Swap, Push and Rotate, TASS

A fixed repertoire of local manoeuvres that provably terminates in polynomial time.

The trade

Solution quality is poor — but they never fail to terminate, which no decoupled planner can promise.

This era is where the four-rung recovery ladder later in this deck comes from: when a general planner is stuck, fall back on rules that always finish.

Explicit link forward. Our recovery ladder is unashamedly rule-based, and it is in the system for exactly the reason this era existed. It used to have seven rungs; truncating it to four measured 6% better, which is slide 32.

History · era 4 of 5

Lifelong: the warehouse changes the question

Kiva / Amazon Robotics, 2008

Hundreds of robots, narrow aisles, tasks that never stop arriving. Wurman, D'Andrea & Mountz.

MAPD and Token Passing, 2017

Ma, Li, Kumar & Koenig formalise pickup-and-delivery as a continuous stream.

RHCR, 2021

Li et al. replan a rolling window, resolving collisions only within the horizon.

Token Passing, its task-swapping variant TPTS, and RHCR are the three baselines this project measures itself against — each reimplemented from its own paper.

Commercial pressure, not theory, produced this era. Worth saying: the reframing was forced by a real floor, and that is also why the objective changed from makespan to throughput.

History · era 5 of 5

Scalable: one timestep at a time

PIBT — Okumura, Machida, Défago & Tamura

IJCAI 2019; Artificial Intelligence, 2022. Priority inheritance and backtracking, resolved within a single timestep. This is the algorithm this repository extends.

LaCAM, 2023

Builds a complete search on top of PIBT, recovering guarantees without giving up its speed.

Thousands of agents, in real time.

PIBT is the whole basis of this project, so give it a beat. The key insight to plant here: it never plans more than one timestep, which is why the 5^k problem never arises.

The turn that made this project necessary

From one-shot MAPF to lifelong MAPD

Classical MAPF

k agents, k goals, fixed in advance.
Plan once, offline, then execute.
Success is every agent parked on its goal.
Objective: makespan or sum-of-costs.
The plan is valid until it finishes.

Lifelong MAPD

Tasks arrive as a stream — Poisson, bursty or scheduled.
A robot that delivers is immediately re-assigned.
There is no terminal configuration to plan to.
Objective: throughput, service time, and its tail.
Every plan is provisional; you replan forever.

The line that matters most is the last one on the right. Because every plan is provisional, a mechanism that is expensive to compute has to justify itself every single timestep. That is the cost argument that eventually deleted five of the nine score terms.

The core we build on

PIBT, in four steps

  1. Candidates

    The four neighbours of the current cell, plus staying put. At most five. Nothing longer than one step is ever planned, which is why the 5k problem never arises.

  2. Hard rejection

    Off the graph, vertex conflict, swap with the robot that pushed me. Nothing else — a rule that deletes a legal move breaks the argument that a legal joint move always exists.

  3. Score and sort

    Survivors are ranked by S(v): progress, lane, turning, crowding. Every preference in this system acts here, and only here.

  4. Push, then backtrack

    If the best cell holds a robot that has not planned yet, that robot plans next, inside this call. If it cannot move, undo the claim and try the next candidate.

Cost per timestep O(|A|(Δ(G) + F + log|A|)). Measured at 1.19 ms/step for warehouse_bottleneck, single-threaded pure Python.

Step 2 is the one to emphasise. Everything this project added lives in step 3, as a number; the review that produced this deck was largely the story of finding mechanisms that had quietly moved themselves into step 2, where they could delete a robot's only way out.

The core we build on

Pushing, in one picture

R1 moves aside, so R3 moves, so R7 moves — all in the same timestep. If nobody can, the chain unwinds and R7 tries its second-best cell.

Nothing copies a priority number here. Inheritance is structural: the pushed robot is made to plan inside the caller's call frame, and is forbidden from swapping back into the cell the caller is leaving. The priority numbers only decide the order of the outer loop.

This is the mechanism the whole deck turns on, and the footer corrects a thing the name gets wrong. "Priority inheritance" sounds like an assignment; in the code it is a recursive call with the caller passed as parent, and the only thing the parent confers is the swap prohibition.

The chain only works because R1 has somewhere to go. Delete one of R1's legal moves — which is exactly what a hard one-way rule does — and the chain dead-ends, R7 never moves, and the layer that deleted the move gets blamed for the congestion it caused. That is not a hypothetical: it is what this project spent a year doing.

The core we build on

And when the push fails: unwind, and stay put

Staying put is always available, so the recursion always terminates and a legal joint move always exists. That is the whole safety argument — and it is why no preference in this system is ever allowed to delete a candidate.

Say the last line slowly. Every design decision in the rest of the deck follows from it: a score may make a move expensive, never impossible, because the termination argument is "there is always somewhere to go, even if it is here".

Computation 1 of 3 · task assignment

What one robot-and-task pair costs

J(i,τ) = 1.0 · d(robot → pickup) + 0.5 · d(pickup → delivery) + 12.0 · congestion
− 0.5 · min(waited, 60) + 1.0 · bottleneck fraction

Distance, then the trip

Getting to the pickup counts full; the delivery leg counts half, because that distance is paid whoever takes the task — it separates tasks, not robots.

Congestion, at weight 12

The fraction of cells on the route that are occupied, plus the load on the aisles it crosses. Twelve because it has to be able to out-argue a few cells of distance.

Waiting, at −0.5, capped

The one negative term: the longer a task has waited the cheaper it gets, so nothing is left in the queue forever. Capped at 60, or in a lifelong run it eventually dwarfs everything else.

The cap is not a detail. Uncapped, waiting time grows without bound and the match degenerates to oldest task first within about a hundred steps — which is why congestion-aware assignment could never show an effect before it was capped.

If someone asks why the delivery leg is discounted rather than dropped: it still separates a short task from a long one, which matters when two robots are equidistant from two pickups. Halving it says "this matters, but less than getting there".

A term estimating delay from one-way aisles used to sit in this function. It went with the aisle-direction layer.

Repeated global minimum — not Hungarian, not an auction. It is O(n·m) a round and it does not produce the optimal matching: watch what the last robot ends up with.

The point of the third round is the honest one. The last robot does not get its own cheapest task — that one was taken in an earlier round by a robot that wanted it more. A Hungarian matching would have found the assignment that minimises the total; greedy finds a good one in a fraction of the time, and in a lifelong run the matching is redone every timestep anyway, so a mistake costs one step rather than a whole plan.

That argument is worth making explicitly if anyone asks why not Hungarian: the optimal matching of a snapshot is not the optimal policy over time, and the snapshot changes before the matching finishes mattering.

When it goes wrong · 1 of 3

What counts as being stuck

Progress means the route distance to the waypoint strictly decreased this step. Not that the robot moved — a robot shuffling sideways round an obstacle is not making progress, and a robot that moves back and forth is making none at all.

10 steps

of no progress, or of being blocked, before the robot counts as stalled at all.

Reset, not stalled

Idle robots, and robots already standing on their waypoint, are zeroed rather than counted.

In dense lifelong traffic a robot waits several steps as a matter of course. So no progress is a precondition, never a trigger — which is the next slide, and the single largest effect in the sensitivity study.

The distinction between "did not move" and "did not get closer" is worth a sentence. Blocked time counts the first; the no-progress counter counts the second, and the second is the one that catches a robot being helpfully pushed in the wrong direction over and over.

When it goes wrong · 2 of 3

A stall only counts when something corroborates it

A wait-for cycle

Built from the edges the push recursion recorded last step: I asked this robot to move. A cycle in that graph is a real deadlock, and its members corroborate each other.

A repeated configuration

The last four positions equal the four before them. The robot is not stuck — it is busy, in a loop, which is worse, because it looks like activity.

Turning corroboration off — escalating on the precondition alone — measures −54.1% throughput (p < 0.001, 10 seeds, 4 maps). It is the second largest effect of any parameter in the system, and it is a switch that only decides when to believe a stall.

The reason this matters so much is that recovery is destructive. It reroutes robots and sends them to escape vertices; doing that to a robot that was merely queueing costs a delivery and makes the queue worse. Half the throughput of this planner is in not panicking.

The wait-for graph is one step stale, because detection runs before planning. That is deliberate and worth conceding if asked: a cycle that existed last step and has already cleared is a false positive the ten-step precondition filters out.

When it goes wrong · 3 of 3

Four rungs, one per timestep — and two of them do nothing

  1. Recompute the routes from scratch

    Works. Cheapest thing that can help, so it goes first.

  2. Raise the blocked robots' priorities

    Inert. The next line of the timestep recomputes priority from scratch and overwrites it.

  3. Allow temporary reverse movement

    Inert. Reversing was never forbidden — the kinematics check returns false on every branch.

  4. Send the group to escape vertices

    Works. Nearest free bay, or a junction with three ways out.

Why this is on a slide rather than in a bug tracker

Rungs 2 and 3 have been dead for the whole measured history of this planner. Every number in this deck was produced by a ladder that is really two rungs and two no-ops — and it still recovers most groups, because rung 1 is doing the work.

The ladder used to have seven

The last three released aisle permits, dropped directional constraints, and hijacked waypoints. They went with the layer they served. Truncating measured +6%.

One rung per timestep, not all four at once: in a synchronous loop you cannot observe whether a remedy worked until the next step, so firing them together would apply three of them blind.

Expect to be asked why the dead rungs are still there. The honest answer is that removing them is a change to measured behaviour — rung 2 and rung 3 each consume a timestep before the ladder reaches rung 4, so deleting them makes recovery reach the escape-vertex remedy two steps sooner, and that is a thing to measure, not to assume. It is the top item on the future work slide.

The RECOVERY state is terminal, which is a second defect worth conceding if it comes up: nothing takes a robot out of it, so a robot sent to an escape vertex keeps its task and can never complete it.

RHCR shares no such assumption and is the honest comparison of the three.

The animation in docs/gifs/ is this exact configuration — same map, robot count, arrival rate and seed — so the picture and this number are the same run rather than two things that resemble each other.

Sixty parameters became thirty-seven. The one that hurt most to delete was the one the whole project was named after — and the measurement that killed it, p = 0.95, is about as close to "no effect whatsoever" as a number gets.

Every deletion here was preceded by a measurement, and every measurement is in docs/data/ with the git SHA of the code that produced it. Nothing was removed because it looked untidy.

The entry-permit number is the one that should sting. A mechanism that was costing a third of the planner's throughput sat in the system for the whole of its measured history, because it was never ablated on its own — it shared a flag with the direction layer, so the two were only ever measured as a bundle.

The general lesson, if there is one: an ablation flag that controls more than one mechanism is not an ablation. It is a bundle, and its effect size cannot be attributed to anything.

What these numbers do not say

Four caveats, stated rather than buried

Every scenario is saturated

corridors releases about 390 jobs in 400 steps and the best planner clears about 60. So throughput measures the capacity of the floor, not responsiveness, and the queue grows without bound behind every planner.

The baselines are outside their envelope

Token Passing and TPTS assume a well-formed MAPD instance; warehouse_bottleneck does not provide one at sixteen robots. That is stated, not scored.

Four maps, and pooling hides disagreement

A knob can help on one map, hurt on another, and pool to zero. The full table carries a worst-map column for exactly that reason.

No multiple-comparison correction

Twenty-three knobs at α = 0.05 means roughly one false positive is expected. The load-bearing findings are far past that; the marginal ones are suggestive only.

And one more, on recovery: turning it off raises throughput 4.2%, but jams then go unresolved — 6.4 a run on corridors against 0.3 — and p95 service time gets worse. It was kept. That is a trade-off, not a free win, and it is on this slide rather than in a footnote.

Put this slide before the future-work slide, not after. An audience that has heard the caveats from you does not have to go looking for them, and every one of these is the first thing a careful reviewer would ask.

Where this goes

Next, in order of expected payoff

  1. Delete the two dead recovery rungs, and measure it

    Rungs 2 and 3 each burn a timestep before the ladder reaches the one remedy that works. Removing them is a change to measured behaviour, so it needs a run, not an assumption.

  2. Give the RECOVERY state a way out

    Nothing transitions a robot out of it. A robot sent to an escape vertex keeps its task and can never complete it, which is a leak on every jam the ladder reaches rung 4 on.

  3. Work out why the additions lose on open grids

    They are decisive on corridors and a net loss on narrow and medium. A layer whose payoff depends on the map should be able to tell which map it is on.

  4. Pair each baseline with the assignment policy from its own paper

    Assignment is currently held constant to isolate the movement layer. That is the right experiment for our question and the wrong one for "is RHCR good".

Item 4 is the one a reviewer will press on, and it should be conceded early rather than defended: our baseline numbers answer "which movement layer is better", not "which published system is better".

In closing

Three things worth taking away

  1. The core was the contribution all along

    Forty years of MAPF converged on a mechanism — push the robot in your way, and unwind if it cannot move — that a hundred lines of recursion capture. Plain lifelong PIBT still wins on the open grids.

  2. A score may make a move expensive, never impossible

    Every collapse this project ever blamed on congestion turned out to be a preference that had been allowed to delete a candidate. The termination argument is "there is always somewhere to go" — and every deleted move is a place there might not be.

  3. Measure the mechanism, not the bundle

    The layer this project was named after measured −0.3%, p = 0.95. It survived for years because it was never ablated on its own.

End on the third point if there is time for only one. The most transferable lesson here is not about warehouses: it is that a mechanism which has never been measured alone has never been measured.
